    Chapter 1: Digital Design and Timing Fundamentals

    About 20 minutes · The base layer: every other question builds on this

    1.1 Setup and hold: the two equations

    • Setup: Tclk + Tskew ≥ Tcq + Tlogic(max) + Tsu + uncertainty, where Tskew = capture clock arrival minus launch clock arrival. Violating it means the data arrives too late. Fix by reducing logic depth, pipelining, retiming, upsizing cells, or restructuring. Slowing the clock also works but costs performance.
    • Hold: Tcq + Tlogic(min) ≥ Thold + Tskew (same skew definition). Violating it means new data races through before the capture flop has finished sampling. It does not depend on clock period, so a slower clock does not fix it. Fix with delay buffers (usually inserted during physical design).
    • Slack = required time minus arrival time. Negative slack means a violation. WNS is the worst negative slack; TNS is the sum of all negative slacks.

    Try it: setup and hold slack calculator

    Skew here means capture clock arrives later than launch clock (positive skew): it helps setup and hurts hold. Try raising skew and watch which slack moves in which direction.

    1.2 Metastability

    If data changes inside the setup/hold window, the flop output can sit at an in-between voltage and take an unpredictable time to resolve. It cannot be eliminated, only made rare enough. Mean time between failures:

    MTBF = e(Tr/τ) / (T0 × fclk × fdata)

    Here Tr is the resolution time you give the signal (one full clock period per extra synchroniser stage), and τ and T0 are technology constants. The exponent is why each extra flop in a synchroniser improves MTBF enormously, and why a lower clock frequency helps.

    1.3 Coding style that survives synthesis

    • always_ff with non-blocking (<=) for sequential logic; always_comb with blocking (=) for combinational logic. Mixing them causes simulation vs synthesis mismatches and races.
    • Latches are inferred when a signal is not assigned on every path (missing else, incomplete case). Fix with default assignments at the top of the block. always_comb and lint will flag this.
    • unique case tells tools the cases are mutually exclusive (parallel mux); priority case keeps the order (priority logic). Use unique only when it is truly one-hot, otherwise simulation and silicon can disagree.
    • Reset: asynchronous assert, synchronous deassert is the common recommendation. Async assert works even with no clock; sync deassert avoids recovery/removal violations and flops leaving reset in different cycles.
    • Beware X-propagation: uninitialised flops, case defaults assigning X, and X-pessimism in gate-level simulation.

    1.4 FSM design

    TopicWhat to say
    Moore vs MealyMoore outputs depend only on state (glitch-free, one cycle later). Mealy outputs depend on state and inputs (faster response, but input glitches and combinational paths from input to output).
    EncodingBinary (fewer flops), one-hot (faster, simpler decode, more flops; common in FPGA and speed-critical logic), Gray (when states change one bit at a time matters, for example power or CDC).
    SafetyAlways cover unused states with a default that returns to IDLE. Consider an error state for illegal conditions.
    StyleOne block for state register, one for next-state logic, one for outputs (registered outputs preferred to ease timing).

    1.5 Reset synchroniser and clock gating

    Why the latch? If en changed while clk was high, a plain AND gate would produce a truncated or glitching clock pulse. The latch holds en steady during the high phase. In real flows you use the library ICG cell, not hand-written gating.

    Deeper: clock skew, uncertainty and OCV

    Skew is deliberate (useful skew) or accidental. Uncertainty in SDC models jitter and margin. OCV/AOCV/POCV derate launch and capture paths differently to cover on-chip variation. Common-path pessimism removal (CPPR) takes back the margin on the shared part of the clock tree. You do not need the maths at Round 1, but be ready to say what each one is for.

    Self-check quiz

    Q1. Your design fails hold at one corner. Does lowering the clock frequency help? What do you do?

    No. Hold depends on the data path delay versus clock skew and flop hold time, not on the period. Fix by adding delay (hold buffers) on the short path, usually in physical design, or by reducing positive skew. Also check the RTL for paths that are accidentally too short.

    Q2. Why use non-blocking assignments in sequential blocks?

    Non-blocking assignments update at the end of the time step, so all flops sample the old values, as real hardware does. Blocking assignments in clocked logic can create order-dependent races and simulation vs synthesis mismatches.

    Q3. A signal is assigned only inside if (en) in an always_comb block. What happens?

    A latch is inferred (always_comb lint will warn). Add a default assignment or an else branch so the signal is assigned on every path.

    Q4. Why is "asynchronous assert, synchronous deassert" preferred for resets?

    Assertion works immediately, even without a running clock. Deassertion is aligned to the clock, so every flop leaves reset in the same cycle and there are no recovery/removal violations or metastability on release.

    Q5. Moore or Mealy for a registered control interface, and why?

    Moore (or a Mealy with registered outputs), because outputs are glitch-free and there is no combinational path from input to output, which makes timing closure and CDC easier. Mealy is chosen when you need to react in the same cycle.

    Key takeaways

    • Setup depends on the clock period; hold does not.
    • Latches come from incomplete assignments; use defaults.
    • Non-blocking for flops, blocking for combinational logic.
    • Reset: async assert, sync deassert. Register FSM outputs.
    • Metastability cannot be removed, only made very rare.

    Chapter 2: RTL Coding Drills

    About 20 minutes · Practise writing these on paper. Interviewers look at structure, edge cases and how you explain your thinking.
    How to answer a coding question: (1) restate the spec and ask about reset, widths and corner cases, (2) sketch the block diagram, (3) write the code in clean, synthesisable style, (4) test it with two or three examples aloud, (5) mention timing and area trade-offs.

    2.1 Round-robin arbiter

    Idea: keep a mask of ports that are "after" the last granted port. Do fixed-priority arbitration over the masked requests first; if none are active, fall back to unmasked requests. The trick x & (~x + 1) isolates the lowest set bit.

    Walk-through: req = 4'b1011, last grant was port 1 (mask = 4'b1100). Masked requests = 4'b1000, so port 3 wins. Next mask after granting port 3 is 0 (shifted out), so the unmasked path runs and port 0 wins. That is the wrap-around.

    Follow-ups to expect: weighted round-robin, fairness under bursts, locking a grant until the packet's last beat, and registering the grant to improve timing.

    2.2 Synchronous FIFO with full and empty

    Use one extra pointer bit. Empty when pointers are equal. Full when the lower bits are equal and the extra bit differs.

    Try it: FIFO depth calculator

    Writer sends a burst of B words at rate fw; the reader drains at rate fr (both words per cycle, writer faster than reader). Depth needed ≈ B × (1 − fr/fw), plus margin for pointer latency (2–3 for async FIFOs).

    2.3 Ready/valid pipeline stage (skid buffer)

    Goal: cut the combinational path from out_ready back to in_ready without losing throughput. A second storage slot (the "skid") catches the word that arrives while the output is stalled.

    2.4 Divide-by-3 clock with 50% duty cycle

    An odd divider with 50% duty needs both clock edges: build a posedge waveform and a negedge-delayed copy, then AND them.

    Check by hand: pa is high for two cycles and low for one (period 3). na is the same waveform half a cycle later. The AND is high from the late rising edge to the early falling edge: 1.5 cycles high, 1.5 low. In real silicon, clock dividers are usually built from library cells and constrained with generated-clock definitions in SDC.

    More drills to practise (write each in under 10 minutes)
    • Rising-edge detector and pulse stretcher.
    • Overlapping "1011" sequence detector (Moore and Mealy).
    • Binary-to-Gray and Gray-to-binary converters; Gray counter.
    • Priority encoder and count-leading-zeros.
    • LFSR (x^8+x^6+x^5+x^4+1) and a CRC step.
    • Serial-to-parallel and parallel-to-serial with valid flags.
    • Pipelined adder with valid/ready flow control.
    • Debouncer (counter-based) and a clock-enable generator.
    • Byte-swap by endianness parameter; parameterised width converter (32-to-64).

    Self-check quiz

    Q1. In the round-robin arbiter, what happens if the last granted port was the highest-numbered port?

    The new mask has no bits set (the shift pushes the grant out of range), so the masked request vector is empty and the arbiter falls back to the unmasked fixed-priority result, which grants the lowest-numbered requester. That is the wrap-around.

    Q2. How can you tell a FIFO is full using pointers, and why the extra bit?

    With N-bit addresses, equal pointers could mean empty or full. A pointer one bit wider breaks the tie: equal pointers means empty; equal lower bits but different top bit means the writer has lapped the reader, so full.

    Q3. Why not simply drive in_ready = out_ready in a pipeline stage?

    It creates a combinational path that ripples back through every stage, hurting timing as the pipeline grows. A skid buffer registers the back-pressure at the cost of one extra storage slot, and keeps full throughput.

    Q4. Why is the divide-by-3 output pa & na better built from registered signals?

    A combinational decode of a counter can glitch, and a glitching clock is dangerous. Registered signals change cleanly on an edge. Even then, production designs use library clock cells and generated-clock constraints.

    Key takeaways

    • Clarify the spec first (reset values, widths, back-to-back cases), then code.
    • Arbiter: mask plus fixed-priority; x & -x isolates the lowest set bit.
    • FIFO: extra pointer bit for full/empty; size by burst length and rate difference.
    • Skid buffer: registered ready, one extra slot, full throughput.
    • Odd dividers need both edges; avoid combinational clock logic.

    Chapter 3: Clock Domain Crossing and Asynchronous Design

    About 25 minutes · The posting asks for "solid knowledge of asynchronous design concepts and CDC methodologies". Expect several questions here.

    3.1 The problem and the standard fixes

    When a signal crosses from one clock domain to another with no fixed phase relationship, the receiving flop can go metastable. The scheme you choose depends on what crosses.

    What crossesTechniqueWatch out for
    Single-bit levelTwo-flop synchroniserSource signal must be registered and glitch-free; the level must be held long enough to be sampled.
    Single-bit pulse, fast to slowToggle (pulse) synchroniser: toggle a flop in the source, sync the level, edge-detect in the destinationPulses cannot arrive faster than the destination can recover.
    Multi-bit data, occasionalHandshake (req/ack) or enable-based multi-cycle path: data held stable while a synchronised request goes acrossLatency of several cycles; data must not change until ack returns.
    Multi-bit streamAsynchronous FIFO with Gray-coded pointersDepth must cover the burst plus pointer-sync latency.
    Counter or pointerGray code (one bit changes per step)Only valid when increments are by 1.
    ResetReset synchroniser per domainReset domain crossing (RDC) issues between domains.

    3.2 Asynchronous FIFO

    • Write pointer lives in the write domain, read pointer in the read domain. Each pointer is converted to Gray code, registered, then crosses through a two-flop synchroniser.
    • Full is generated in the write domain by comparing the write pointer with the synchronised read pointer. Empty is generated in the read domain with the synchronised write pointer.
    • Full and empty are slightly pessimistic because of synchroniser latency. This is safe: the FIFO may report full or empty a little late, never wrong in the dangerous direction.
    • Gray-code full condition: top two bits of the pointers are inverted, remaining bits equal.
    • Data goes through the memory array, not through the synchronisers.

    Try it: why Gray code works for pointers

    Move the counter and count how many bits change at each step. If several bits change at once and the destination samples mid-change, it can read a value that was never valid. Gray code changes exactly one bit per step.

    3.3 Common CDC failure patterns (know the names)

    • Missing synchroniser: signal crosses directly.
    • Combinational logic before the synchroniser: glitches can be sampled. Register the source first.
    • Reconvergence: two synchronised bits from the same source combine in the destination; they may arrive one cycle apart, giving a transient wrong combination.
    • Multi-bit bus through separate synchronisers: bits can arrive in different cycles. Use Gray, handshake or FIFO.
    • Fan-out of a synchroniser output to multiple domains, or a synchroniser output used before it is stable.
    • Pulse too narrow for the destination clock to see.
    • Data/control mismatch: control arrives before the data is stable.
    • Reset domain crossing: an asynchronous reset from one domain asserting into logic in another domain mid-operation.
    Connect it to your experience: at Cadence you built a "CDC random stage" model to catch convergence and functional issues. Be ready to say exactly what it did. A common idea is that a simulation model cannot capture metastability, so you randomly insert a one-cycle variation on a synchronised path to expose designs that only work when the crossing resolves in a fixed number of cycles. Use your own words based on what you actually built, and give one real bug it found.

    3.4 Tools and sign-off

    Structural CDC (Spyglass CDC, Questa CDC, JasperGold CDC) finds crossings and checks that each uses a recognised scheme. Functional checks use formal or assertions for protocols such as "data stable while request high". Every waiver needs a written reason; interviewers like to hear that waivers are reviewed, not just used to clear the report.

    Self-check quiz

    Q1. Why can't you pass a 4-bit binary counter through four separate two-flop synchronisers?

    Different bits can be sampled on different destination edges, so you may capture a value such as 0111 to 1000 as 1111 or 0000, which was never valid. Gray code changes one bit at a time, so the sampled value is always either the old or the new one.

    Q2. In an async FIFO, why is it safe that full and empty are slightly pessimistic?

    The synchronised pointer is an older value. For full, the old read pointer is smaller than the real one, so the FIFO looks fuller than it is: the writer might wait a little extra. For empty, the old write pointer makes it look emptier than it is: the reader waits a bit. Neither case causes overflow or underflow.

    Q3. A fast-clock pulse must go to a slow clock domain. What can go wrong with a plain two-flop synchroniser, and what is the fix?

    The pulse may be shorter than a slow-clock period and be missed. Use a toggle synchroniser (toggle in the source domain, synchronise the level, detect the edge in the destination), or stretch the pulse, or use a request/acknowledge handshake.

    Q4. How do you move a 32-bit configuration value occasionally between two domains with no FIFO?

    Hold the data stable, send a synchronised request (single bit) to the destination, capture the data when the request is seen, send a synchronised acknowledge back, and only change the data after the acknowledge. The data bus itself is not synchronised; the handshake guarantees it is stable when sampled.

    Q5. What is reconvergence and how do you avoid it?

    Two signals from the same source cross through separate synchronisers and are combined in the destination. Because each can resolve in a different cycle, the combination may briefly be wrong. Avoid it by synchronising a single encoded signal, using a handshake, or crossing a bus together with a qualifying control.

    Key takeaways

    • Choose the scheme by what crosses: level, pulse, bus, stream, reset.
    • Two-flop sync for single bits; toggle sync for pulses; handshake or async FIFO for buses.
    • Gray code pointers guarantee one bit changes per step.
    • Synchroniser latency makes full and empty conservative, which is safe.
    • Know the failure patterns: missing sync, glitches, reconvergence, RDC.

    Chapter 4: Lint, Synthesis, STA and Low Power

    About 25 minutes · The posting lists "Lint, CDC, Synthesis/STA, Power" as SoC flow expertise.

    4.1 The flow in one picture

    4.2 Lint

    Lint catches structural and coding problems before simulation: inferred latches, width mismatches, multiple drivers, unconnected ports, combinational loops, unsynthesisable constructs, X-assignments, and clock/reset misuse. For an IP owner, the process matters: run lint on every check-in, agree a rule set, and review waivers. Mention Spyglass lint, CDC and low-power checks, which are on your resume.

    4.3 Synthesis

    • Steps: read RTL, elaborate, apply constraints, map to the technology library, optimise for timing, area and power, then write the netlist and reports.
    • Reports to check: timing (WNS, TNS, critical paths), area (by hierarchy), power estimate, unmapped or unresolved items, latches, clock-gating coverage.
    • If a path fails timing: look at the structure first. Reduce logic depth (restructure, balance trees), pipeline, retime across a register, duplicate high-fanout drivers, move late-arriving signals closer to the output, check for an unintended priority chain, then let the tool upsize and buffer.
    • Do not "fix" timing by adding false paths or multicycle paths unless the design truly behaves that way.

    4.4 Constraints and STA

    TermMeaning
    False pathA path that can never be functionally exercised (or is covered by another scheme). Exempt from timing. Overuse hides real bugs.
    Multicycle pathA path that is designed to take N cycles. Setup is relaxed to N, and hold must be moved back to N−1.
    Generated clockA divided or gated clock derived from a master clock, declared so STA propagates it correctly.
    Recovery / removalThe "setup / hold" of asynchronous reset deassertion relative to the clock.
    Clock gating checkThe enable of a clock gate must be stable while the clock is high (for AND-type gating).
    Path groupsGrouping by clock or type so a very bad group does not hide others.

    4.5 Low power

    Dynamic power: P = α × C × V² × f. Static power is leakage, which grows with lower threshold voltage and with temperature.

    TechniqueReducesNotes
    Clock gatingDynamic (clock toggling)RTL enable conditions or tool-inserted ICGs. Check gating enable timing.
    Operand isolation / data gatingDynamic (datapath toggling)Hold inputs when the result is unused.
    Multi-Vt cellsLeakageHigh-Vt where timing allows, low-Vt on critical paths.
    Power gating (power domains)LeakageNeeds isolation cells, retention flops, level shifters, power switches, and a wake-up sequence.
    DVFS / multi-voltageDynamic (V²)Needs level shifters and safe frequency/voltage sequencing.

    Power-down sequence (be able to say it): stop traffic, save state (retention), enable isolation, then switch off power. Power-up is the reverse: power on, wait for stable, reset as needed, restore state, then disable isolation. Getting the order wrong is a classic bug.

    Try it: dynamic power estimate

    Notice how strongly voltage matters: P scales with V². A drop from 0.8 V to 0.7 V saves about 23% of dynamic power.

    Deeper: how power is analysed

    Power tools combine the netlist, parasitics and switching activity (SAIF, VCD or FSDB from simulation or emulation). Vectorless estimates use default toggle rates and are only a rough guide. Report peak, average and leakage, and compare against the budget. Power-aware simulation (UPF-based) checks isolation and retention behaviour in RTL and gate-level.

    Self-check quiz

    Q1. When is a multicycle path legitimate, and what is the hold setting?

    When the design guarantees data is launched less often than every clock (for example, a register updated once every N cycles with an enable, with the capture flop sampling only at the Nth edge). Set setup to N cycles, and hold to N−1 so hold is checked against the right edge. Without the hold adjustment, the tool would demand an enormous hold fix.

    Q2. Why is a false path on an asynchronous crossing sometimes the wrong choice for a Gray-coded bus?

    A false path removes all timing checks, so skew between bits is unconstrained, which can break the one-bit-changes assumption. A set_max_delay -datapath_only keeps the skew within a bound while ignoring clock relationships.

    Q3. A critical path has a long priority if/else if chain. What would you try?

    Check if priority is really required; if the conditions are mutually exclusive, use a parallel structure (unique case, AND-OR mux). Otherwise restructure as a tree, or pre-compute conditions in the previous cycle (pipelining).

    Q4. Order the power-down sequence for a switchable domain.

    Quiesce the domain, save state (retention), enable isolation, then cut power. Power-up: restore power, wait for the supply to settle, release reset as needed, restore state, then disable isolation.

    Q5. Clock gating or power gating: which reduces leakage?

    Power gating. Clock gating stops toggling, so it saves dynamic power only; the cells still leak.

    Key takeaways

    • Flow: lint and CDC, then synthesis, STA and power, with constraints and UPF as inputs.
    • Fix timing in the RTL structure before relying on tool effort or exceptions.
    • Multicycle: setup N, hold N−1. False paths hide real bugs if overused.
    • Dynamic power depends on activity, capacitance, V² and frequency; leakage needs power gating or high-Vt.
    • Isolation and retention order matters in power-down and power-up.

    Chapter 5: Protocols (AXI/APB, Coherency and CHI, DDR/LPDDR)

    About 30 minutes · Plus the Micron-specific blocks named in the posting: LPDDR subsystem, CPU subsystem, SoC test controller.

    5.1 APB and AXI

    ProtocolUseKey points
    APBLow-speed registers and peripheralsTwo phases: SETUP (PSEL=1, PENABLE=0), then ACCESS (PENABLE=1) until PREADY=1. PSLVERR reports errors. At least 2 cycles per transfer. No pipelining.
    AHBOlder mid-speed busAddress phase overlaps previous data phase; HREADY stalls; single master per layer.
    AXI4High-performance memory-mapped5 independent channels (AR, R, AW, W, B), valid/ready on each, bursts, IDs, outstanding transactions, out-of-order across IDs.
    AXI4-LiteControl registersNo bursts, single beat, simple.
    AXI-StreamData streamsNo addresses; TVALID/TREADY/TLAST.

    The valid/ready rules (they ask this)

    • A source must not wait for READY before asserting VALID. It asserts VALID when data is available.
    • Once VALID is asserted, it must stay asserted and the payload must stay stable until the handshake (VALID and READY both high on a clock edge).
    • READY may be asserted before VALID, and may depend on VALID. A transfer occurs on any edge where both are high.
    • Avoid combinational loops: do not make VALID depend combinationally on READY, and avoid READY and VALID depending on each other in the same cycle through logic.

    Try it: valid/ready handshake checker

    Tick VALID and READY for each clock cycle. Green cycles are transfers. Red means VALID was dropped before READY (a protocol violation).

    AXI facts to have ready

    • Burst types: FIXED, INCR, WRAP (wrap lengths 2, 4, 8, 16). AXI4 INCR bursts up to 256 beats.
    • 4 KB rule: a burst must not cross a 4 KB address boundary.
    • Ordering: responses with the same ID return in order; different IDs can be reordered. AXI4 has no write interleaving.
    • Write channel: AW and W are independent channels; B response comes after the last write beat.
    • Responses: OKAY, EXOKAY, SLVERR, DECERR.
    • Outstanding transactions hide latency; the number you support affects buffering and area.

    5.2 Cache coherency basics

    When several agents cache the same memory, the system needs a protocol so every agent sees a consistent value. Two common mechanisms: snooping (broadcast; simple, does not scale) and directory or snoop-filter based (track which agents hold a line; scales better). Your IBM PowerBus work is snoop-based coherency, with ownership tracked in a CAM.

    Try it: MESI state machine for one cache line in one core

    Apply processor events (this core) or snoop events (other cores) and watch the line state.

    Current state: I (Invalid)
    StateMeaning
    M, ModifiedOnly copy, dirty. Memory is stale; must supply or write back on snoop.
    E, ExclusiveOnly copy, clean. Can write silently (E to M) with no bus traffic.
    S, SharedPossibly other copies, clean. A write needs an upgrade (invalidate others).
    I, InvalidNot present.

    5.3 AMBA CHI

    • States: I (invalid), UC (unique clean), UD (unique dirty), SC (shared clean), SD (shared dirty). Compare with MESI: UC≈E, UD≈M, SC≈S, and SD allows a dirty line to be shared.
    • Typical requests: ReadShared (want a readable copy), ReadUnique (want to write: others invalidated), CleanUnique (already have data, want ownership), MakeUnique (will overwrite whole line), WriteBack, Evict, and non-snooping ReadNoSnp / WriteNoSnp.
    • Flow of a ReadUnique miss: RN-F sends the request on REQ to the HN-F; HN-F checks the snoop filter; sends snoops (SNP) to sharers; owner returns data (DAT) or HN-F fetches from SN-F; HN-F sends Comp with data; RN-F sends CompAck so the home node can release the address.
    • Why the home node orders: serialising all requests to one cache line at its home node is what keeps coherency simple; the cost is tracking conflicts and blocking (a point to mention for deadlock avoidance).
    • Deadlock avoidance: separate channels for REQ, RSP, SNP, DAT, with dependency rules so a response can always make progress.
    Mapping to your experience (say this honestly): "I have production experience with IBM PowerBus coherency, including snooping and CAM-based ownership tracking, in a block that handles I/O coherency on the Nest fabric. I have completed structured training on CHI and am studying CMN-700. The concepts map directly: my PAU behaves like an I/O-coherent requester (conceptually an RN-I/RN-D), PowerBus snooping corresponds to the snoop channel, and my ownership CAM does the job of conflict and ownership tracking that a CHI home node does." Do not claim you designed CHI nodes unless you did.

    5.4 DDR and LPDDR

    • Organisation: channels, ranks, bank groups, banks, rows, columns. DDR4 has 16 banks (4 groups of 4); DDR5 has 32 banks (8 groups of 4), burst length 16 and two independent sub-channels per DIMM, plus on-die ECC.
    • Commands: ACTIVATE (open a row), READ/WRITE (column access), PRECHARGE (close the row), REFRESH.
    • Key timings: tRCD (activate to column), CL (read latency), tRP (precharge), tRAS (min row open), tRC = tRAS + tRP, tRFC and tREFI (refresh time and interval), tFAW and tRRD (limits on activates), tCCD_L/S (column to column, same or different bank group).
    • Scheduler: prefers row hits (FR-FCFS), balances fairness and QoS, groups reads and writes to reduce bus turnaround, interleaves across banks and bank groups, and inserts refreshes.
    • Address mapping and striping: mapping bits to bank, bank group and channel decides parallelism. Your CMN600-compliant remap and striping work is exactly this.
    • ECC: SECDED on the bus; chip-kill style symbol correction (your RSECC work); metadata carried with data.
    • DFI: the standard controller to PHY interface (commands, write/read data, update and training handshakes).
    • Training: write leveling, read gate and DQ/DQS training, command/address training (LPDDR), ZQ calibration, run by the PHY or by controller firmware at boot.
    • LPDDR4/5 vs DDR4/5: LPDDR is low voltage, narrower channels (x16 per channel), multiplexed command/address, burst 16, extra power states and fast frequency switching; LPDDR5 adds a separate write clock (WCK) and link ECC options.

    SoC test controllerThe block that manages test and debug modes: a JTAG TAP (IEEE 1149.1), test-mode control, scan and MBIST controllers, and access to internal test wrappers (IEEE 1500). Interview point: test modes must not break functional timing; test clocks and resets need care.

    Self-check quiz

    Q1. Can an AXI slave deassert READY after asserting it, before VALID arrives?

    Yes. READY may be asserted or deasserted freely before VALID. The restriction applies to VALID: once asserted it must stay high with stable payload until the handshake completes.

    Q2. What is the difference between E and M in MESI, and why does E exist?

    E is a clean unique copy, M is a dirty unique copy. E lets a core write without any bus transaction (E to M silently) because it knows no one else has the line. Without E, the first write to a privately read line would always need an invalidation broadcast.

    Q3. What role does the home node play in CHI?

    It is the point of coherency for a range of addresses: it orders requests to the same cache line, uses a snoop filter or directory to decide which caching agents to snoop, collects data from a cache or from the memory controller, and manages the completion handshake. It may also contain a system-level cache.

    Q4. Why are row hits preferred in a DRAM scheduler, and what limits that preference?

    A row hit needs only CL, while a conflict needs tRP + tRCD + CL. Always preferring hits can starve other requests, so schedulers add age limits, QoS priorities, and fairness across masters. Refresh and write/read turnaround also interrupt hit streams.

    Q5. What does "CPU subsystem" mean on a memory ASIC job description?

    Integrating a processor core with its surrounding logic: wrapper, interrupts, local memory, debug, clock and reset, and bus connections, plus handling CDC, power and timing at the interface. It does not mean designing the core pipeline.

    Key takeaways

    • Valid/ready: once VALID is up it stays up with stable data until the handshake.
    • MESI/CHI: know the states, who supplies data on a snoop, and why E avoids traffic.
    • The home node orders and snoops; map your PowerBus work to it honestly.
    • DRAM: hit < empty < conflict in latency; scheduling balances hits, fairness and refresh.
    • Micron specifics: LPDDR subsystem, CPU subsystem (integration), SoC test controller (JTAG, scan, MBIST).

    7.3 One-page cheat sheet

    Timing and design
    • Setup: Tclk ≥ Tcq + Tlogic + Tsu + skew + uncertainty. Hold: Tcq + Tmin ≥ Thold + skew. Hold is independent of period.
    • Latches come from incomplete assignments. Non-blocking in clocked blocks.
    • Reset: async assert, sync deassert.
    CDC
    • Level: 2FF. Pulse: toggle sync. Bus: handshake or async FIFO. Pointers: Gray code.
    • Failure patterns: missing sync, glitch before sync, reconvergence, multi-bit through separate syncs, RDC.
    STA and power
    • Multicycle: setup N, hold N−1. Use set_clock_groups for async clocks; bound Gray buses with max-delay.
    • P = αCV²f. Clock gating: dynamic. Power gating and high-Vt: leakage.
    • Power-down: save, isolate, power off. Power-up: power on, reset, restore, release isolation.
    Protocols
    • AXI: VALID must not wait for READY; stays up with stable payload until the handshake. 4 KB boundary. Same ID in order.
    • APB: SETUP then ACCESS, PREADY, minimum 2 cycles.
    • MESI: E to M is silent. CHI: RN-F, RN-I, HN-F, SN-F; REQ, RSP, SNP, DAT; states I, UC, UD, SC, SD.
    • DRAM: hit = CL; empty = tRCD + CL; conflict = tRP + tRCD + CL.
